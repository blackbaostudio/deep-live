Legal certainty

Buying in Spain means buying with guarantees: a European Union country, the euro as its currency, and a reliable land registry system in which every property has a public, verifiable history before you sign. For someone investing far from home, that predictability is worth as much as the price.

Spain answers the question of why Europe. The next question is a natural one: within Spain, why the Costa Blanca?

Block 02

The Valencian Community

The Costa Blanca answers the question of where. And at the center of it all is Alicante.

We are not talking about a lost village or a huge, impersonal metropolis. Alicante is a city on a human scale, with the sea inside the city, the services of a provincial capital, and an international airport fifteen minutes away. The right balance between a calm life and staying connected to the world.

  • •The sea inside the city. Postiguet beach is right by the center, with temperatures between 45°F in winter and 88°F in summer.
  • •A center with soul. The Santa Cruz quarter, the Explanada de España by the port, the marina, and Santa Bárbara castle atop Mount Benacantil.
  • •A city for walking. In ten minutes on foot you are at your destination. Here you recover something that in the United States has almost been lost.

The Costa Blanca, beyond the city

Less than an hour from the airport, each town has its own character: Altea, a white old town above the sea. Calpe, the Peñón de Ifach rock and a well-established international community. Jávea and Dénia, coves and pine woods by the sea. Benidorm, services all year round. Santa Pola and Torrevieja, a fishing-town feel and more accessible entry prices.

Valencia, the best city in the world to live in as a foreigner

A little over an hour away is Valencia, the third-largest city in Spain. The InterNations city ranking, which every year surveys tens of thousands of expats around the world, has repeatedly placed it in first place worldwide for living as a foreigner. It is not because of any single thing, but because of how they all add up: you get around on foot, it is affordable for a major capital, it is safe, it has a beach inside the city, and healthcare and services of the highest level.

It is also a city to look at. The Gothic Lonja de la Seda, a World Heritage Site, sits alongside the modernism of the Mercado Central, one of the largest fresh-produce markets in Europe, and the contemporary architecture of the Ciudad de las Artes y las Ciencias by Santiago Calatrava, today an international icon of the city.

Where the Turia river once ran there are now five and a half miles of gardens crossing the city from end to end, the largest urban park in Spain. Together with the Malvarrosa beach, the farmland that surrounds it, and a mild climate all year round, it explains why those who visit do not forget it.

And it has an intense street life. The Fallas, recognized as Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, fill March with ephemeral art, fireworks, and celebration. Paella was born here, and the culture of Sunday family rice dishes is still part of the calendar.

It is no coincidence that it has become popular among buyers from northern Europe, Dutch, German, Belgian, and British, and increasingly among Americans who discover that a great European city can still be lived calmly and by the sea. For many of our clients the ideal equation is clear: a home on the Costa Blanca and Valencia as the major reference city a step away.

Living here is easy, in large part, because getting here and getting around is easy.

Block 03

Connectivity

Living well far from home comes down to one very practical thing: how easy it is to come and go.

Alicante has its own international airport and is connected by high-speed rail to the main transatlantic airport in Spain.

From plane to train, without leaving the terminal

Madrid-Barajas is one of the great transatlantic hubs in Europe, with direct flights to New York (about 9 hours), Boston, Miami, and Chicago. From the airport itself you connect to the high-speed train, which reaches Alicante in 2 hours and 20 minutes, with around fourteen trains a day each way.

A night flight and a morning train. You leave home in the afternoon and you are having lunch in Alicante the next day.

Spain has the largest high-speed rail network in Europe and the second-largest in the world, behind only China, with close to 2,500 miles in service.

Getting around without a car

The center, the old town, the Explanada, the port, and Postiguet beach are all connected on foot. The TRAM light rail runs along the coast above ground toward San Juan beach, El Campello, Villajoyosa, Benidorm, and Dénia.

And there is a question that, sooner or later, we all ask when we think about living somewhere else: healthcare.

Block 04

Healthcare

It is the first question almost every American client asks, and the one with the most reassuring answer in the whole dossier.

Spain has one of the most efficient healthcare systems in the world and one of the highest life expectancies on the planet, around 84 years, the second among the major economies behind only Japan.

2nd

life expectancy in the world

12.8 million

people with private insurance

There is private health insurance from €60 to €90 a month. The most complete policies go up from there depending on age and coverage. Even in the most expensive scenario, we are talking about figures that in the United States would correspond to a few weeks of equivalent coverage.

But the price is not what matters. What matters is the other thing.

What really changes

In Spain, if you need medical care, they treat you. That is all.

There is no need to check first whether the hospital is in your insurance network, or to negotiate coverage, or to wait weeks afterward for a bill with staggering figures for something that has already happened. There is no possibility of being ruined by an emergency, or ending up in debt over a treatment, or having to take out a loan to have surgery.

It is the same with education: affordable prices and no debt behind them. For many of our American clients, this is the part of the change of life that weighs most over time. It is not a saving, it is no longer having a worry.

On the Costa Blanca

The province of Alicante has spent decades caring for a very large international community. Care in English is common in the private sector along the coast, and there are pharmacies in every neighborhood with price-regulated medicines, far below US prices.

In Spain, getting sick does not ruin anyone.

Homicide rate per 100,000 people. Spain has roughly one-seventh of the US rate, and is below the European average.

Put in everyday terms: here people walk at night, children go to school on their own, you leave the terrace door open, and the conversation about safety is not part of family routine.

A peaceful country, and it always has been

Spain is a safe country and has been so consistently. It is not a streak or a recent improvement: it is a structural feature of how life works here. It ranks 25th in the world in the 2025 Global Peace Index.

  • •Firearm ownership is exceptional and heavily regulated. There is nothing resembling the everyday gun violence of the United States.
  • •It is a country deeply opposed to war and armed conflict. It is a very settled social stance, cutting across society and visible in the street. Spain has not seen a conflict on its territory in generations.
  • •Institutional stability. A member of the European Union, with the euro as its currency and a predictable legal system.

What crime there actually is

What exists is essentially against property and without violence: petty theft, pickpockets in tourist areas, and break-ins at empty homes out of season. It is prevented with normal measures, and with someone you trust keeping an eye on the house while you are away.

It is not that nothing ever happens here. It is that safety stops being something you have to think about every day.

Block 06

Living in Spain: legal, tax, and financial

This is the part that usually stops people. It should not, and least of all in your case.

To buy a property in Spain you do not need a visa, or residency, or to live here.

You can buy today, use the house a few weeks a year, and decide much later, or never, whether at some point you want to spend more time here.

And the city is about to change shape

Alicante has spent a century split in two by the railway tracks. Block 09

Tourism and lifestyle

And now, the important part: what an ordinary Tuesday is like.

Spain received 96.8 million international tourists in 2025, an all-time record and the second-most-visited country in the world, behind only France. If the whole world chooses to come, there must be a reason. And for those who buy here there is a second reading: housing on the Spanish coast is backed by constant international demand that sustains the value of the asset.

Figures for the province of Alicante. The Blue Flags are more than half of all those in the Valencian Community. The Michelin stars are spread across 13 restaurants.

Golf

Fifteen courses affiliated with the Costa Blanca Golf Courses Association, open twelve months a year thanks to the climate. Las Colinas Golf has been named the best course in Spain five times at the World Golf Awards. Alicante Golf, on San Juan beach, was designed by Severiano Ballesteros and preserves the remains of a Roman villa from the first century BC.

Playing here in January, in shirtsleeves, is an experience an East Coast client understands immediately.

Gastronomy

The province brings together 17 Michelin stars in 13 restaurants, among them Quique Dacosta, three stars in Dénia, and BonAmb, two in Jávea. But what really changes daily life is not that: Alicante is a land of rice dishes, of seafood with the fish markets of Santa Pola and Dénia half an hour away, of Jijona nougat, and of a culture of tapas and markets that happens every day.

Festivals all year round

There is no dead season here, and much of the year has its festival. In June, Alicante throws itself into the Hogueras de San Juan, declared a Festival of International Tourist Interest: monuments that burn in the street, fireworks, and open-air nights to welcome the summer. The Moros y Cristianos festivals move through the towns of the province in spring and summer, and an hour away, in March, the Fallas of Valencia go up in flames.

The rest of the year keeps pace. In winter, between 59 and 68°F many days, you play golf, walk on the beach, and eat on the terrace. And autumn is the best-kept secret of the coast: warm sea, good light, and no crowds.

A good climate, you get used to. A good life, you do not.
